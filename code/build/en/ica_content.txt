TITLE|From Stars to Pull Requests: Validity Boundaries along the Agent Skill Diffusion Chain
RUN|VALIDITY BOUNDARIES IN THE AGENT SKILL CHAIN
H1|Abstract
P|Diffusion research routinely treats stars, installs, forks, and pull requests as interchangeable proxies for attention, adoption, and contribution, yet their interchangeability is rarely tested. This article proposes the concept of a validity boundary: a platform metric is valid only for the segment of the diffusion chain to which it belongs, and ceases to be valid once used to proxy the next construct. Using the Agent Skill ecosystem as a case (35,947 repositories, 155,547 documents, 1.85 million forks), with hypothesis tests run on a 341-repository adjudication panel, the article tests two segments—attention-to-installation and fork-to-pull-request—supplements a third, mid-chain segment (copy-to-fork) with a different-source sample, and reports descriptive evidence for the remaining segments. The rank correlation between stars and installs is .204, excluding |ρ| ≥ .50; the first-return rate of forks is 6.33%, with 87.8% of first returns within 24 hours—a workflow signature; the copy–fork supplement yields ρ = .107, likewise excluding medium-or-stronger association. Horizontally, segments carry almost no information about one another (normalized mutual information of .022 or less); dynamically, weekly stars and pull requests move together within repositories (median ρ = .37), which argues against pure measurement noise. Three boundaries observed in three samples suggest that coupling is segment-dependent, but a chain-level claim is not made. The article does not assert structural coupling and does not extrapolate to the long tail.
KWD|validity boundary; diffusion chain; platform metrics; segment-by-segment test; Agent Skill ecosystem
H1|1. The Chain Presumed Continuous: Proxy Convenience and the Interchangeability Hypothesis
P|Diffusion research since Rogers (2003) treats adoption as a decision that follows awareness and persuasion, and treats platform traces as the residue that decision leaves behind. Computational practice has converted that sequence into a chain of substitutable indicators—stars proxy attention, downloads proxy installation, commits and pull requests proxy participation—so that adjacent platform metrics can stand in for one another. The conversion is a measurement convenience rather than a theoretical claim, yet it is rarely examined head-on. Cultural-market experiments documented how unpredictable collective attention is (Salganik, Dodds, & Watts, 2006); news-cycle research showed that most content lives briefly (Leskovec, Backstrom, & Kleinberg, 2009); structural-virality analyses established that large cascades run shallow (Goel, Anderson, Hofman, & Watts, 2016). All of these works still assume that the error introduced by substituting one metric for the next construct is local and calibratable.
P|That platform metrics are not diffusion itself is nearly a commonplace. This article asks a more specific question: if the proxy chain is not one process sliced into stages, what exactly does each metric measure, and at which link does substitution produce an identifiable misjudgment? Answering requires a field where attention, installation, copying, contribution, and review are simultaneously observable. The ecosystem of Agent Skills—repositories of packaged instructions and tools that extend AI agents—provides such a field, and it warrants communication research for a second reason: what circulates there is not code alone but instructions for conduct, so the objects being counted sit closer to discourse than to software.
P|The article first establishes the validity boundary as a testable concept: a platform metric’s validity ends at its home segment and fails beyond it. The concept comprises three components—attribution, endpoint, and positionality—with corresponding evidence, and is tested at two links: attention–installation and fork–pull request (PR). The concept also corrects the existing substitution chain: prior work glosses fork→PR as “adoption→contribution,” but a fork is first a technical act. Jiang et al.’s (2017) motivation study recorded two usages—archiving a copy and submitting a PR—and in this ecosystem the technical usage dominates. We therefore restate the chain as attention → installation → copying → fork → PR → review and test it segment by segment. On this basis the tests condense into two interpretive threads: the star–install separation speaks to medium bias and the audience commodity; the rapid fork–PR return cycle speaks to platform workflows as strategic rituals. Review and attribution enter as measurement descriptions, and curation serves as an out-of-chain check. We write “interpretive” rather than “generative mechanism” deliberately: observational data can display patterns but cannot identify processes.
P|A scope statement is required. Of the six segments, only two receive supported-tier tests, for two reasons. First, in the 341-repository panel, 315 (92.4%) have zero cross-repository copies; only 26 are nonzero, too few to estimate an installation-to-copy correlation. Second, copy-to-fork is likewise not judgeable within the panel; this article supplements it instead in a different-source subsample of repositories that do have cross-repository copying edges (Section 4.3). P3 and P4 are therefore positioned as measurement descriptions; adjudication of chain morphology awaits follow-up. The title marks the chain’s coverage, not the tests’ coverage: conclusions fall only on the tested segments.
P|The proxy chain is not without ancestry. Audience-measurement history supplies the template: ratings proxied viewing, circulation proxied reading, and their validity problems grew alongside the commercial measurement institutions that produced them. Ang (1991) showed that the measured “audience” is itself a construction of the measurement institution; Napoli (2011) made the commodified evolution of audience measurement an object of audience research in its own right; Webster and Ksiazek (2012) traced attention flows across media networks, noting that fragmentation means any single indicator captures only one side of behavior. These works, however, still handle “one indicator–one construct” validity. The diffusion chain lines multiple indicators up as a sequence of proxies. This article generalizes the problem to the chain version: each link’s validity is tested independently, and interchange must be proven link by link.
P|The article makes three contributions. (1) It proposes “validity boundary” as an operational test framework for chain-style metric substitution, specifying attribution, endpoint, and positionality with corresponding adjudication. (2) It provides demonstration tests at two segments—two one-sided tests (TOST) of equivalence excluding medium-or-stronger correlation at attention–installation, and hazard-rate morphology identifying a workflow signature at fork–PR—plus a different-source supplementary test at the mid-chain copy–fork segment. (3) The three boundaries suggest that coupling along the diffusion chain may be segment-dependent—the chain is neither wholly invalid (attention–fork remains far more strongly associated than any other pair, NMI = .523) nor continuously valid—though the generality of this suggestion awaits same-source tests at intermediate segments, and the article does not offer it as established chain-level theory. Three claims: metric validity ends at its home segment; adjacent segments fail the interchange threshold; boundary positions are set segment by segment. Three non-claims: chain-wide weak coupling; boundary extrapolability; wholesale invalidity of platform metrics.
H1|2. Validity Boundaries: Attribution, Endpoint, and Positionality
H2|2.1 Three literatures that do not connect
P|Diffusion studies treat adoption as a decision following awareness and persuasion, with platform metrics as traces the process leaves behind. Platform studies show that platforms transcribe behavior into rankable quantities and allocate visibility accordingly, but debate the allocation far more than whether the quantities substitute for one another (Rogers, 2018; van Dijck, Poell, & de Waal, 2018; cf. Borges, Hora, & Valente, 2016). Measurement research asks whether an indicator measures the construct it claims (Cronbach & Meehl, 1955; Messick, 1995) and how heterogeneous things are rendered into a common scale (Espeland & Stevens, 1998). All remain at “one indicator–one construct” relations; none addresses multiple indicators proxying one another in sequence. This article extends construct validity from one-to-one correspondence to chain-style proxying.
H2|2.2 Three levels to distinguish
P|(a) Diffusion theory’s own proposition: adoption is a decision that follows awareness and persuasion (Rogers, 2003). (b) Computational proxy practice: stars proxy attention, downloads proxy installation, commits proxy participation—measurement convenience, not theoretical claim (van Dijck et al., 2018). (c) This article’s falsification target: the proxy-interchangeability hypothesis—adjacent segments’ signals should correlate strongly enough to substitute. We falsify the implicit assumption of measurement practice, not Rogers-style diffusion theory. For level (c), five propositions each pair with a testable null; “low correlation” is never a finding in itself—only “too low to interchange” adjudicates.
H2|2.3 The concept
P|A validity boundary is the point at which a platform metric’s validity for its claimed construct ends. Attribution: every metric first belongs to a communicative segment and records that segment’s behavioral trace, not the chain as a whole—forks belong to branching workflows rather than participatory intent (Jiang et al., 2017); review records belong to procedural continuity rather than gatekeeping validity. These are attribution questions, not precision questions. Endpoint: past the home segment, the metric’s association with the next construct falls below an interchange threshold; we adopt |ρ| = .50 as a reference, borrowing the medium-effect convention of the construct-validity literature (Campbell & Fiske, 1959; Cronbach & Meehl, 1955; Messick, 1995)—a conventional, not substantive, choice; P1 reports sensitivity over .35–.60, and at .30 exclusion fails. Positionality: boundaries do not appear uniformly along the chain but at particular segments; two are tested here, while attention–fork remains strongly associated (NMI = .523)—the chain is not wholesale invalid.
H2|2.4 Against discriminant validity, stated head-on
P|The concept must be compared with discriminant validity explicitly, or it will be read as a rename. The two share one judgment: a measure should not correlate excessively with what it should theoretically be distinguished from; Fornell and Larcker (1981) operationalize exactly this by comparing the square root of average variance extracted with construct correlations. Three differences follow. Object: the multitrait–multimethod matrix estimates convergence and discrimination simultaneously across a trait × method layout, judging which constructs should be distinguished; this article juxtaposes adjacent segments of a chain, judging where proxying stops. Premise: MTMM requires traits and methods to be exchangeably ordered; this article assumes diffusion segments have structural order—upstream indicators precede downstream ones, and the downstream construct has its own indicator. Meaning of failure: discriminant failure indicts the instrument and is repairable by changing the method; a boundary is an indicator–construct misplacement between segments and is not repairable by any method change. The validity boundary is therefore not an extension of discriminant validity but its conversion from “should or should not be distinguished” to “where does proxying stop”—a chain question.
H2|2.5 Against the validity critiques of computational social science
P|The concept must likewise be positioned against the measurement reflexivity of computational social science. Existing discussions center on the data layer: representativeness and platform bias, gaps between traces and behavior (Lazer et al., 2009; Ruths & Pfeffer, 2014; Tufekci, 2014); construct–indicator mismatch when digital traces are read as networks (Howison, Wiggins, & Crowston, 2011); and GitHub-specific data traps—mirror, empty, and renamed repositories that understate activity (Kalliamvakou et al., 2014)—to which this article’s fork-rename matching and install-estimation caveats belong. These literatures ask whether the data represent the phenomenon they claim; this article asks whether the indicator proxies the next construct on the chain. Data validity and chain-proxy validity are orthogonal. The contribution is thus not new data but the extension of construct validity from “one indicator–one construct” to chain-style proxying, with a portable segment test.
H2|2.6 Boundaries are not measurement error
P|Error arises within a construct and can be weighted or calibrated; a boundary arises between constructs, where no weighting repairs “having measured the wrong thing.” The distinction is operational rather than semantic. Classical test theory can disattenuate a correlation for unreliability—Section 4.1 reports exactly this correction—but disattenuation presupposes that both indicators measure the same construct; applied across a boundary, it would inflate a correlation that should not exist in the first place. Reliability concerns the precision of a reading; a boundary concerns the identity of what is being read.
H2|2.7 Weak coupling as a to-be-tested configuration
P|“Weak-coupling configuration” is a form proposition under test, not an established chain-level property. It requires three conditions simultaneously: at least two adjacent segments fall below the interchange threshold; the horizontally weakly coupled series nevertheless co-move dynamically, ruling out pure noise; and boundaries are non-uniform, with at least one adjacent segment remaining strongly associated. P1 and P2 satisfy the first two; attention–fork’s NMI = .523 satisfies the third; with only two same-source test points, the article reports a “configuration suggestion,” not establishment. The concept is not Weickian loose coupling (Orton & Weick, 1990; Weick, 1976)—elements that respond to one another yet retain their identities at the organizational level—but its measurement-level analogue: indicator–construct correspondence that ends at a segment. Nor is it a test of Luhmann’s (1995) structural coupling.
H2|2.8 Two scales of coupling
P|Horizontal coupling asks whether conversion rates correlate across repositories—does a repository’s rate co-vary with its stars, installs, or channel visibility? Dynamic coupling asks whether within-repository weekly fluctuations co-move—do weeks with larger star gains also see more PRs? The two can dissociate: horizontal coupling reflects between-unit processes; short-term fluctuation can be driven by common shocks. Horizontal evidence carries the main adjudication; dynamic co-movement serves a discriminating function—if low correlation were noise, the series should not co-move. This division underwrites P2 and the integrative analysis in Section 5.
H2|2.9 Expectations and propositions
P|P3 and P4 are measurement descriptions; P5 is out-of-chain; tier positions are summarized at the end of Section 4. P2’s expected direction follows a classic proposition. Tuchman (1978) described news production routines as strategic rituals: practitioners need not judge whether an outcome is achieved, only whether the procedure was completed. If fork→PR bears such procedural traces, first returns should be front-loaded in time and unrelated to attention—precisely what P2’s null anticipates. Theory here performs no causal identification; it supplies falsifiable time-signature expectations. Four segment tests and one out-of-chain check follow:
P|P1: Stars and installs should not reach the interchange threshold, |ρ| = .50; and a skills.sh hit should not predict a ClawHub hit once stars and repository age are controlled.
P|P2: If forks carry sustained contribution intent, first returns should show a continuous, deep-familiarity time structure with conversion rising in repository heat; if workflow-driven, returns should be sharply front-loaded and attention-independent.
P|P3: If copying erodes attribution, attribution survival in copy pairs should not exceed independent-pairing expectations; if a template attribution convention, survival should approach the production baseline and direction should be unjudgeable.
P|P4: If review is effective, merge outcomes should be predictable from observable features (AUC significantly above .50); if only formal survival, coverage should be low and prediction weak.
P|P5: If curation is a heat market, stars should predict curation; if power is insufficient, the verdict is insufficient evidence, not support for a boundary.
P|Five pre-specified tests and one supplementary check are reported in three evidence tiers—support, description, insufficient—never substituting significance flags for effect sizes.

H1|3. Data and Identification: Four Layers, Four Denominators
H2|3.1 Four nested layers
P|Data run through October 24, 2026, in four nested layers, each carrying its own sub-denominators (Table 1). The ecosystem-scan layer comprises two rounds of topic- and search-based collection covering 35,947 unique skill repositories. The content layer comprises 155,547 SKILL.md full texts, each SHA-256 hashed with SimHash near-duplicate detection (a 300-pair audit: character 5-gram Jaccard median = 1.00, minimum = .838; precision guaranteed, recall lower-bounded only). The panel layer comprises 341 repositories completing five-channel queries—stars, skills.sh, ClawHub, release downloads, npm: 172 with skills.sh install hits, 83,215 ClawHub registry entries, release downloads above zero for 131 repositories, npm packages for 84. The reciprocity layer contains 1,851,928 forks across 228 parent repositories with complete timestamps; 225 parents fully parsed (1,832,187 forks, 122,235 return forks, 447,495 return PRs). The governance layer lists 45,203 PRs across 189 repositories, 44,572 connectable to GraphQL review data; 23,988 merged-and-review-connectable PRs serve review-coverage estimation.

H2|3.2 Four rules and identification strategies
P|Four rules apply. (1) Two fork-conversion denominators: the main test uses all forks (risk model); structural description uses return forks; the two are not mixed. If return forks’ all PR events form the denominator, 37.0% fall within 24 hours and 52.0% beyond 7 days—describing post-return sustained submission, not first return. (2) Missing is not zero: installs, reviews, and fork timestamps are handled as missing. (3) Author identity by login aggregation; multi-accounts, organizational accounts, and CI service accounts cannot be separated; bot-status is inferred from login patterns (bot, action, sync) as an auxiliary classification only. (4) P1’s equivalence bound is |ρ| = .50 with two one-sided tests (TOST) at α = .05; no multiplicity correction applies because this is a single pre-specified primary test; SimHash uses a 64-bit fingerprint with Hamming distance of 3 or less defining near-duplicates (recall of 93.0% or better).
P|Measurement boundaries are reported alongside. The 341 panel repositories are not a probability sample but an adjudication sample prioritized by platform visibility (0.95% of the ecosystem), applicable to “the head already inside the platform’s field of vision,” not the long tail. Analysis samples are overlapping subsamples drawn from the same population under different conditions—172 install-hit, 228 with full fork data, 189 with PR lists, 266 non-curated for curation—and the four denominators do not sum. Install counts are platform-reported values whose window and deduplication rule are unpublished; they are treated as a single count, neither deduplicated users nor executions. Platform metrics render heterogeneous objects commensurable and rankable (Espeland & Stevens, 1998)—but commensurability is precisely what the segment tests examine. P4’s review coverage uses merged-and-connectable PRs (23,988) as denominator, which entails selection bias (unmerged and unconnectable PRs excluded); an all-PR lower bound is reported as a directional sensitivity analysis without Heckman correction, and correlational conclusions remain descriptive.
P|Analytic strategies: P1 uses Spearman rank correlation with Fisher-z intervals, 90% CIs for equivalence inference and 95% CIs for description, and TOST equivalence testing against |ρ| = .50, reporting within-stratum point estimates rather than substituting within-stratum significance for population estimation. P2 uses Kaplan–Meier cumulative incidence over all forks and discrete-interval hazards, event = first fork→PR, censoring = data cutoff; intervals of less than 1h, 1–24h, 1–7d, 7–30d, 30–90d, 90–365d, and more than 1y; a second population in conversion rates is assessed via GMM (k = 1–4, BIC) and kernel-density modes. P3 compares attribution survival across 4,000 near-duplicate copy pairs against an independent-pairing expectation built from field marginals; template homogeneity and within-repository clustering may inflate the expectation, so the 77.8% versus 9.7% contrast is an upper-bound comparison. P4 uses AUC with five-fold repository-grouped cross-validation and permutation tests (null .50), fold intervals and BH-corrected q. P5 uses a logit odds ratio with 95% CI and minimum-detectable-effect reporting; at n = 266, interval width is itself the power constraint. The integrative analysis quartile-discretizes four repository-level variables into five-level information with 500 permutations, checks 3–6 bin sensitivity, and reports distance correlation for continuous variables to avoid bin-boundary artifacts. Dynamic coupling uses within-repository Spearman correlations with two-way (repository × week) fixed effects to absorb common shocks, with repository-clustered standard errors. All tests are pre-specified; except P4’s merge prediction (BH q), each has a single primary test, and secondary analyses are reported as exploratory.

H1|4. Segment-by-Segment Tests: Support, Description, and Insufficient Evidence
P|We report the two supported segments, one supplementary different-source check, the measurement descriptions for later segments, and one out-of-chain check, closing with the evidence-tier table.
H2|4.1 Attention → installation (P1)
P|Across the 172 install-hit repositories, the stars–installs Spearman correlation is ρ = .204 (95% CI [.056, .343]; 90% CI [.080, .321]), p = .007 (Figure 2a). Under the equivalence bound of |ρ| = .50, both one-sided TOST tests reject (p = 4.2 × 10⁻⁶), supporting an |ρ| < .50. This does not prove “no correlation”; it shows medium-or-stronger correlation can be excluded. Within star quartiles, ρ = .12, .28, .37, .31; within age terciles, .17, .37, .19—directionally consistent, but strata are small and intervals wide. We cannot claim within-stratum zeros; we can claim that every within-stratum point estimate lies below .50 and the overall interval’s upper bound is below .50 (Figure 2b). Stratification did not drive the correlation to zero; it located it inside the weak-correlation band.
P|Channel independence also fails: controlling stars and age, a skills.sh hit predicts a ClawHub hit (logit b = .71, p = .017). The two platforms are not independent marketplaces but share upstream signals. P1’s verdict: boundary supported, limited to excluding medium-or-stronger correlation, and lower-bound evidence. When the equivalence bound varies from .35 to .60, TOST one-sided p falls from 2.0 × 10⁻² to 1.3 × 10⁻¹⁰ with the verdict unchanged; tightening to .30 yields p ≈ .09—insufficient to exclude. No attenuation correction is applied; the true correlation may exceed .204, but the directional verdict holds under present measurement conditions (classical reliability .6 implies corrected ρ ≈ .34; reliability .5 implies ≈ .41; only reliabilities below ≈ .41 would cross .50). Cross-stratifying by age and star medians (273 days; 7,660 stars), the new × low, old × low, and old × high cells all exclude an |ρ| ≥ .50 (TOST p < .001, .021, .002). The new × high cell is the exception (n = 31, ρ = .477, p = .438): it carries no verdict, and we report it only to mark where a future same-source test with more high-star new repositories should look.
P|Stars are also a metric that can be bought. Purchased or coordinated stars add variance to the star measure that is uncorrelated with installation, which attenuates ρ in the same way random measurement error does; unless manipulation were systematically targeted at repositories with high installs, the observed .204 is more likely an over- than an under-estimate of the association between genuine attention and installation. The exclusion of medium-or-stronger correlation therefore survives, but the point estimate should not be read as a clean attention–installation elasticity. The strongest available evidence comes from Shen, Sood, and Weitzel (2026), who bought stars for a random set of GitHub repositories and found no detectable effect on downloads, forks, pull requests, or issues. Their design identifies the effect of added stars on adoption; ours concerns the association between standing stars and installs. The two are compatible, and both point away from a strong star–install coupling.
P|Dynamically, the evidence is exclusionary, not confirmatory: co-movement indicates construct difference, but co-movement itself does not prove a boundary—the supporting evidence remains the cross-sectional TOST.
P|If stars are read as expressed attention and installs as actual use, P1’s separation re-instantiates, in a platform ecosystem, the ratings–use gap that measurement institutions once maintained: in the television era the gap between ratings and behavior was sustained by the measurement regime; here it is sustained by the behavior itself. Gerlitz and Helmond (2013) call the comparable, countable, cumulative reaction button the platform’s currency device—the “like economy”; the star is its counterpart in the skill ecosystem: the most commodifiable, hence most displayable, signal in the attention market (Webster, 2014). This reading supplies interpretability, not causal identification: it explains why stars are the most conspicuous metric, and the most detachable from installs, without attributing causal contribution to either side.

H2|4.2 Fork → PR (P2)
P|For first-return risk, the all-fork risk model looks markedly different from the return-fork-only view. Of 1,851,928 forks, 117,169 returned before cutoff: cumulative incidence 6.33%—4.82% within 1 hour, 5.55% within 24 hours, 6.09% by 30 days, 6.36% by one year (Figure 3a). Among returners, 87.8% of first returns occur within 24 hours (median 0.01 days); fewer than 5% occur after 7 days. Interval hazards fall from 4.82% (under 1h) to 0.09% (over 1y) (Figure 3b)—an order-of-magnitude drop after the first hour. This is the signature of “fork completes the branch action immediately,” not “deliberate, familiar contribution.”
P|Three figures must be kept apart: 6.33% is the ever-return proportion (117,169 of 1,851,928); 6.36% is the one-year Kaplan–Meier incidence with censoring weights; 6.67% is the fork-layer return rate (122,235 of 1,832,187; denominator = forks of the 225 fully parsed parents). Denominators differ; directions agree. Recomputed as first returns, 87.8% fall within 24 hours and fewer than 5% after 7 days: temporal truncation strengthens, not weakens, front-loading; long-term returns are a minority of high-frequency authors’ sustained submissions.
P|Structural description reinforces the workflow reading. Fork-layer conversion is 6.67%, per-repository median 3.95%; excluding the 12 repositories with conversion at or above 20% (5.3% of parents) yields 4.79% (median 3.16%); excluding the top 5 by event volume yields 4.53% (median 3.40%). The typical repository converts at roughly 3.2%–3.4%; 6.67% is a fork-weighted value lifted by a few large repositories. Conversion is unrelated to star scale: across star deciles, per-repository median conversion fluctuates between 2.6% and 5.3% with no monotonic gradient, and the regression of log conversion on log stars is negligible (β = −.01, p = .81). The distribution is unimodal and heavy-tailed: GMM’s BIC selects k = 1 on both logit and log₁₀ scales (649.4 versus 655.5; 649.4 versus 657.3), KDE unimodal; the 12 repositories at or above 20% are tail, not a second population (a bimodality coefficient of .742 superficially suggests bimodality but is notoriously heavy-tail-sensitive). The tail is curated- and engineering-driven: curated repositories’ median conversion closely matches non-curated (3.57% versus 3.90%), yet their probability of entering the 20% tail is an order higher (21.1% versus 2.1%). For curated lists, fork→PR is the submission interface—change a line, open a PR is the established workflow; it does not turn readers into contributors.
P|Event concentration clarifies what “contribution” is in these data: the top 5 parent repositories (hermes-agent, openclaw, playwright, dify, awesome-mcp-servers) account for 46.8% of 447,495 return PRs; of 99,798 authors, 483 (0.48%) contribute 38.9% of events and the top 1% contribute 46.5% (Table 3). High-frequency authors are not bots (bot-pattern logins: 0.97% of events); playwright’s five maintainers cluster in one repository, twenty’s two in another. Forking is a team’s branch-development workflow, not a stranger’s participation entrance.

P|Taken together, P2’s verdict: fork’s validity as a proxy for contribution intent is unsupported; what the data identify is narrower—the fork timestamp does not mark the onset of contribution intent. The boundary therefore holds for fork as a time marker, and only indirectly for fork as a construct measure. This section tests fork→PR conversion, not “adoption→contribution”; the reason is not that contribution intent is absent but that this layer chiefly measures workflow and the submission interface. When copying already delivers the full artifact, a fork is first a technical act; reading it as participatory commitment mistakes the workflow for the commons (Benkler, 2006). The verdict also explains why conversion is attention-independent: two different processes align—one the platform’s attention allocation, the other team branching plus curated-list submission.
P|A fast return is compatible both with “workflow-triggered” and with “intent before fork”: a skilled contributor may decide on the modification before forking, forking being a necessary technical step whose delay reflects process efficiency, not absent intent. These data cannot separate the interpretations; doing so would require PR content-change volume, pre-fork activity histories, or contributor surveys, none of which are included. P2’s verdict should therefore read: fork→PR conversion’s time morphology is closer to a workflow signature than to a delayed adoption–contribution sequence; the morphology does not exclude contribution intent—it shows the fork itself is not a reliable time marker of intent. Curated repositories’ evidence likewise shows only that high-conversion repositories’ conversions are driven by an established workflow, not that intent is absent—curated-list submitters already know they will file a PR, compatible with “intent before fork.”

H2|4.3 Copy → fork: a supplementary test on a different-source sample
P|One more mid-chain segment is testable. Cross-repository copy out-degree—the number of distinct SHA-256 hashes of a repository’s documents that appear as copies in other repositories—can be contrasted with fork counts. The sample comprises repositories with cross-repository copying edges, a population distinct from the 341-repository panel; metadata were supplemented via GraphQL (2,170 succeeded, 294 deleted). Among the 784 repositories with both out-degree above zero and forks above zero, the Spearman correlation is ρ = .107 (p = .003), and the one-sided TOST against an |ρ| = .50 rejects decisively (p < 10⁻¹⁰): medium-or-stronger association can be excluded. As a self-check, the same batch’s fork–star correlation is recovered at ρ = .914, confirming the linkage pipeline is sound. This constitutes a third boundary, located mid-chain. Two caveats govern its status. First, conditioning on both counts being positive truncates the joint range and would, if anything, attenuate the observed association, so the directional conclusion is conservative. Second, the sample is not the 341-repository panel; the result is reported as supplementary and is not folded into the support tier.
H2|4.4 Attribution and review: measurement morphology of the later segments (P3–P4)
P|P3 and P4 do not constitute supported-tier tests; their function is to describe the measurement morphology of the chain’s later segments. Copying and attribution: across 4,000 near-duplicate copy pairs, child-side attribution survival where the parent carries an attribution field is 77.8% (95% CI [76.5, 79.1]) against an independent-pairing expectation of 9.7%; among 68 exact copies, survival is 97.1%. The gap shows attribution strongly co-occurs with copying but cannot identify direction—copying may select already-attributed documents (selection), or attribution may survive copying (retention); both mechanisms generate the same co-occurrence. Because the independent expectation is built from field marginals (template homogeneity and within-repository clustering can inflate it) and no same-template non-copy control exists, P3 is descriptive only; the article retains “consistent with a template attribution convention (to be tested); erosion direction unjudgeable.” File history adds a second side: of 544 judgeable file-sides, 71.3% never carry an attribution field, 26.8% are attributed at birth, and only 1.8% later; of 154,292 frontmatter documents, 83.0% lack attribution. The two denominators differ (342 copy-pairs’ judgeable sides versus all documents) and must not be mixed. Only 8 pairs have both parent–child creation times and attribution write-times judgeable—insufficient to prove “production-side decides.”
P|Review and merging: as a governance practice (Meyer & Rowan, 1977; Shoemaker & Vos, 2009; White, 1950), formal review is sparse: 33.2% of 45,203 PRs have at least one formal review record (including COMMENTED); among the 23,988 merged-and-connectable, 80.0% carry no reviewDecision while 37.6% have at least one review record—having a record is not having a decision; the proportions share a denominator but differ in meaning. Unmerged-closed PRs show 30.7% record coverage, open PRs 19.5%. Three denominators must be separated (all PRs; 44,572 connectable; 23,988 merged); using merged as denominator overstates “reviewed-then-merged” because rejected PRs are absent. Decision coverage rises with repository PR volume—5.8% (under 50), 11.2% (50–199), 16.0% (200–999), 26.2% (1,000 or more)—but stays below 30% (Figure 4a); coverage here means a non-null reviewDecision, excluding COMMENTED-only. Merge authority is highly concentrated: among 108 repositories with at least 20 merges, the head-merger share has median 1.0; 64.8% of repositories (95% CI [55.4, 73.2]) execute 90% or more of merges from a single account. Prior work predicts PR acceptance from content features (Tsay, Dabbish, & Herbsleb, 2014); this article uses repository metadata only, and merge outcomes are only weakly predictable: AUC = .595 (95% CI [.545, .644]; five-fold repository-grouped CV; permutation p = .005; BH q = .008; Figure 4b). The interval excludes .50, so the ranking is not random, but .595 falls far below what “effective review” would require; calibration and Brier scores are not estimated, so .595 must not be read as accurate probabilistic prediction. P4 is adjudicated descriptive, with the two conclusions (low decision coverage; weak predictability) reported separately and “merge concentration” offered as interpretation, not tested mechanism.

H2|4.5 Stars and curation: an underpowered out-of-chain check (P5)
P|If platform curation is a heat market, stars should predict listing. Curated repositories’ median stars (7,587) are lower than non-curated (11,710); the difference is insignificant (Mann–Whitney p = .12). In a logit excluding curated repositories (n = 266), the star coefficient is −.06 (OR 95% CI [.65, 1.35]). At 80% power and α = .05, the minimum detectable OR is approximately 1.69: odds ratios between .59 and 1.69 cannot be excluded. Moreover, 48% of the top-50 uncurated repositories are clearly non-skill repositories, consistent with a category-boundary reading. Brighenti (2007) establishes visibility as a category of social theory—being seen, being watched, and being regulated are separable mechanisms (see also Thompson, 2005): curation here performs the first gate—listed repositories acquire the qualification of being seen, while unlisted heads are placed outside the category. That 48% of unlisted heads are non-skills shows this gate is not a pure quality ranking but carries a category boundary: gatekeeping precedes ranking. This is a descriptive verdict; it supports no inference about curation mechanisms. P5’s verdict: insufficient evidence—neither “stars predict curation” nor “no predictive power” is supported.

H1|5. Segment Dependence: Implications and Limits
H2|5.1 Diffusion research: operationalization error and two propositions
P|Quartile-discretizing four repository-level variables (stars, forks, contribution rate, curation), the contribution rate’s NMI with attention, fork, and visibility is .009, .022, and .012 (permutation p = .802, .156, .569), while attention–fork NMI is .523; 3–6 bin and continuous distance-correlation checks agree. Horizontally, contribution rate carries almost no information about the other three layers; attention and fork remain two measurements of one construct. Yet within the same repositories’ weekly series, star increments and PR events correlate at a median of .374 (212 repositories) and .169 (12 high-conversion repositories); with two-way fixed effects the de-meaned elasticities are .319 (SE .022) and .604 (SE .140). Week fixed effects absorb common shocks, and the elasticity survives—dynamic co-movement is not merely ecosystem-wide exposure—but the high-conversion group has only 12 repositories and a wide interval. Weak coupling is adjudicated horizontally; dynamic co-movement discriminates noise; the division of labor differs. No lagged or reverse-causal tests are performed; dynamic results are exploratory.
P|Contributor demography explains why co-maintenance is weak: 62.4% of authors contribute once yet produce only 13.9% of events; 483 authors with 100 or more contributions produce 38.9%, and the top 1% produce 46.5% (Merton, 1968). The author-event tail index is α = 2.03, against which Simon’s (1955) single-process benchmark implies ρ = 1 − 1/α ≈ .51, while the observed authors-to-events ratio of .223 implies α ≈ 1.29: newcomer entries are largely rejected, and repetition is dominated by high-frequency authors. The “contribution community” is, in fact, one-time contributors plus a thin maintainer core running team workflows; measuring community health by submission volume misreads high-frequency workflow as broad participation. This structure is homologous with the two-step flow’s opinion-leader distribution (Katz & Lazarsfeld, 1955) and consistent with the empirical calibration of the participation ladder (Preece & Shneiderman, 2009): a tiny minority accounts for the bulk of events while most appear once. The difference is that this article’s threshold event is a PR, not a comment—the heavier the threshold, the steeper the ladder—precisely the misreading the participation-ladder literature warns against.
P|Diffusion theory presupposes the attention→adoption→use→contribution sequence; computational research operationalizes it as interchangeable metrics. We show at least two segments are non-interchangeable and boundary positions are uneven. This does not negate stage models; it identifies the measurement slippage that occurs when stage models are operationalized. Two derived propositions follow, distinct from the tested propositions above: H1, the lower the copying cost of a segment, the more likely a boundary; H2, the more standardized the workflow, the more front-loaded the fork–PR time signature.
H2|5.2 Platform-metrics research: bias and commodification
P|The star–install separation admits a post hoc reading through medium bias and the audience commodity (Innis, 1951; Smythe, 1977). Stars are space-biased visibility markers—short-lived, explosive, portable across platforms (Wu & Huberman, 2007)—that do not carry sustained relations; installs are closer to time-biased traces—objects incorporated into workflows, retained, repeatedly invoked. The two are non-interchangeable not because of measurement error but because the platform folds two biased behaviors into one diffusion narrative. Stars are conspicuous precisely because they commodify most easily into comparable, rankable, displayable “attention stock,” as ratings did for commercial media (Bucher, 2018; Gillespie, 2010; Meehan, 1984).
P|Bias theory can be operationalized further as affordance. Among boyd’s (2010) properties of digital traces, stars mainly load the persist–replicate–search–display face—brief, cumulative, cross-platform; installs mainly load the within-platform retention face—objects incorporated into workflows and repeatedly invoked. Their non-interchangeability is thus two affordances folding different behavioral traces into the same diffusion narrative. This makes “separation change around visibility shocks” more than a rhetorical conjecture: it becomes a testable proposition, P6: if stars are indeed space-biased display signals, then after an exogenous visibility shock (platform recommendation, list inclusion), the coupling of star increments and install increments should rise systematically within the shock window; if the separation is constant, bias theory fails in this ecosystem. The present data contain no shock-event labels; P6 awaits future work. The audience-commodity tradition receives a contemporary footnote here: what the platform sells is comparable, rankable attention, while traces of use remain unpriced inside workflows; the unpaid contribution tail—0.48% of authors producing 38.9% of events—approaches the distribution the free-labor literature describes (Jhally & Livant, 1986; Terranova, 2000). These are interpretations, not tests.
P|Diffusion in these data is closer to content distribution than co-maintenance: content is moved, installed, and synced, but rarely discussed, reviewed, attributed, or co-maintained (Businge, Openja, Nadi, & Berger, 2022). For diffusion theory, the coupling hypothesis fails under near-zero copying costs with information–tool bundling; the boundary conditions are now specified. For gatekeeping theory, review quantity is not review effectiveness—formal review persists while decision coverage stays low. Naming the merge-rights distribution gatekeeping concentration connects P4’s description to platform-governance research: formal review’s ceremonial persistence aligns with the “ritual form” judgment (Gillespie, 2018); a head-merger share median of 1.0 and 64.8% of repositories concentrating 90% or more of merges in a single account quantify concentrated gatekeeping power—governance research treats such distributions as direct observations of governance structure (Gorwa, 2019). Testable proposition: the higher the gatekeeping concentration, the lower the formal review coverage—the two descriptions here are juxtaposed, not causally tested. For measurement, no single platform metric proxies a diffusion construct; validity must be assembled layer by layer (Espeland & Stevens, 1998).
H2|5.3 A portable three-step check
P|Methodologically, the validity boundary yields a portable procedure: (1) locate attribution—which class of behavior traces does the metric record; (2) measure the endpoint—does its association with the next segment’s construct exclude interchange; (3) adjudicate—horizontal low correlation combined with dynamic co-movement marks a construct-attribution rupture rather than measurement noise.
H2|5.4 Scope conditions and limitations
P|Five boundaries constrain portability. First, the sample is an adjudication sample, not a probability sample; ratios do not extrapolate to the long tail. Second, the fork risk model includes 228 parents (3 permanently missing), with first returns keyed by full fork name. Third, conversion is a same-time static ratio that cannot distinguish “fork-then-immediate-PR” from “long-term maintainer.” Fourth, author identity is aggregated by login and cannot separate multi-accounts or CI accounts. Fifth, the design is a single cross-section of one ecosystem on one platform; temporal validity and portability to other metric regimes are untested. On reproducibility and ethics: all data are public GitHub and platform pages; repository names and logins are public identifiers; the article reports aggregates only, does not target individuals, and performs no cross-platform identity linking. Analysis code, variable dictionaries, query terms, the sample-screening flowchart, near-duplicate thresholds, fork-rename matching rules, and review-linkage rules accompany the replication package; fork-rename and install-topping sensitivities appear in supplementary materials. These boundaries do not change directions, but they limit effect-size precision; all ratios are read as in-sample denominators.
H1|6. Conclusion: Test the Chain, Segment by Segment
P|In a skill ecosystem where copying costs approach byte-level observation, this article proposed the validity boundary and used it to test the substitution chain of platform metrics. The results are uneven by design: P1 and P2 support boundaries—stars–installs ρ = .204 (90% CI .080–.321); fork first-return 6.33% with 87.8% within 24 hours; a supplementary, different-source test at the mid-chain copy–fork segment likewise excludes medium-or-stronger association (ρ = .107); P3 and P4 supply measurement description (attribution co-occurrence directionally unjudgeable; review formalism persisting amid low decision coverage); P5 lacks power, counting as neither support nor refutation. The discussion adds one proposed, untested proposition—P6, separation change around visibility shocks—for which the data carry no shock labels. That distribution is itself the point: validity is not a once-and-for-all attribute of a metric but an evidence structure that must be tested and reported segment by segment.
P|The contribution lies not in proving the chain weakly coupled but in supplying an operational test framework and demonstrating boundaries at two segments, with a first different-source indication at a third. The findings suggest coupling may be segment-dependent, but the suggestion’s generality is limited by the number of same-source test points; elevating weak coupling to chain-level theory requires same-sample boundary evidence at intermediate segments—the supplementary copy–fork test offers a first indication but does not license the claim. All ratios carry in-sample denominators; the sample is the head already inside the platform’s field of vision, not the long tail. The adjudication framework is portable to other platform ecosystems, but boundary locations and strengths must be tested segment by segment.
